# Lodestar style guide

12 Oct 2026, @David

## 1. What this guide covers

This guide applies to every word a reader sees in Lodestar: screens, briefs, action cards, the results page and the experience map. It combines the style sheet agreed on 11 Oct 2026 with the word list settled during the 12 Oct 2026 review, when about 200 lines across the Lodestar material failed it.

Use it in three ways:

1. Before writing, check the word list (section 8) for the fixed name of anything you mention.
2. While writing, follow the rule for the kind of text you are writing (sections 4 to 7).
3. Before publishing, run the checklist in section 10.

Where this guide and an older note disagree, this guide wins. Text quoted word for word from the panel's run files is the one exception to the rules, and section 3 explains how to label it.

## 2. The standards behind it

No single published style covers a product like Lodestar, so each kind of text follows the standard that corporate readers already recognize for it.

| Kind of text | Standard | What it gives us |
|---|---|---|
| Labels, headings and buttons | Microsoft Writing Style Guide | Sentence case, no end punctuation on labels, and buttons that are verbs |
| Briefs, stories and cards | Bottom line up front (US Army Regulation 25-50) with GOV.UK plain English | The conclusion and the ask come first, evidence follows, and the text works for a reader who has never seen the method |
| Confidence and judgement | Intelligence Community Directive 203 analytic standards (secondary summary) | Confidence is stated separately from likelihood, and always with its basis |
| Numbers and dates | AP conventions (secondary summary) | Numerals for 10 and above, and dates written as 11 Oct 2026 |

The directive and the AP rules were checked through secondary summaries, not the primary documents.

## 3. Hard rules

These rules come from the project's own instructions. A page that breaks one is not published, whatever its style.

1. **Never name the client.** That covers the company, its segments, its product brands and its people. Write "the OEM" (original equipment manufacturer) and name functions instead of people: the resiliency team, the product owner, Sourcing, Install PMO (project management office), Quality and regulatory. A name check runs before every publish.
2. **Never invent a fact.** A rewrite may only re-say what the source says. If a rule asks for a number or a basis the source does not give, write "Placeholder" or "basis not stated".
3. **Never put words in someone's mouth.** A view the resiliency team reconstructed is labelled as a stand-in, with who wrote it and when.
4. **Describe AI panel members as models.** Each one is "an AI model briefed to act as" a catastrophe analyst, a credit analyst and so on. Never present one as a person with a real career.
5. **Label quoted material as quoted.** Panel answers, the moderator's write-ups and the prompts given to each model are shown word for word. They keep their own wording, including terms this guide bans, and sit under a label such as "Quoted word for word from the run files", with a short key to the panel's working labels nearby.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| (A named client lead) is in every row as the enabler. | The resiliency team is in every row as the enabler. |
| The crux the owner named is the one the run turned on. | The run answered the resiliency team's main doubt. (Low confidence that this reflects the owner: no interview yet.) |
| A catastrophe analyst who has priced physical hazard for insurers for 20 years. | An AI model briefed to act as a catastrophe analyst who has priced physical hazard for insurers for 20 years. |

## 4. Labels, headings and buttons

This covers anything short that sits on a screen: page titles, section headings, field labels, table headers, chips and buttons. The source is the Microsoft Writing Style Guide.

1. Use sentence case everywhere. Capitalize only the first word and proper names. Never set text in all capitals, including through CSS.
2. Headings name the thing. They do not promise what the reader is about to get.
3. Labels have no end punctuation. A field label is followed by a colon or a line break, never by a full stop. Sentences keep their full stops.
4. Buttons are verbs that say what happens.
5. One idea per label. Don't string fragments together with dots or full stops.
6. Name things in words. A screen code or risk code may appear only beside a plain name that the screen explains.

| Rule | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | MR, conventional magnet line. Five lines, then one decision. | MR conventional magnet line |
| 2 | Where the MR magnet line breaks before 2031, and what to do now | MR (magnetic resonance) conventional magnet line: supply risks to 2031 |
| 3 | What it protects. / Why now. / Act. | What it protects / Why now / When to act |
| 4 | Take it | Send to Sourcing |
| 4 | Mark as elicited | Mark interview as done |
| 4 | Confirm. There is one qualified mill. | Confirm one qualified mill |
| 5 | Owner: Sourcing. Due: Q2 2027. Principle: sourcing strategy. | One row each. Owner: Sourcing. Start by: the second quarter of 2027. |
| 5 | Walled · drifting here | The indicators lean toward this future |
| 6 | R1 N16 | The United States magnet plant |
| 1 | THE BRIEF IT WAS GIVEN, WORD FOR WORD | The prompt this model was given, quoted word for word |

## 5. Briefs, stories and cards

This covers any sentence a reader is meant to act on: the bottom line, future descriptions, action cards and screen intros. The sources are bottom line up front and GOV.UK plain English.

1. The first sentence is the bottom line. It says what is happening and what we are asking. Evidence follows. An intro never describes the page itself.
2. Write full sentences. They are short but complete. A fragment is allowed only as a label.
3. Plain words come before method words. If a method word is needed, give its plain meaning first on that screen. Section 8 lists the fixed plain words.
4. Spell out every abbreviation on first use on each screen, in the form MR (magnetic resonance). Each screen counts separately, because a reader may land on any one.
5. Say who does what. Name the function that acts. Avoid a passive that hides the actor.
6. Use one name for one thing. The same outage, part or team keeps the same name on every screen.
7. Check facts across screens. Counts, dates and owners must agree everywhere they appear.

| Rule | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | This page shows when the action has to start and what waiting costs. | Sourcing must start qualifying a second wire mill by the second quarter of 2027. |
| 1 | MR is magnetic resonance. Two uncertainties decide which future we get. | Two questions decide which of four futures the MR (magnetic resonance) line faces by 2031. |
| 2 | Helium cover at the United States magnet plant. / Cold heads. / By 2029. | The United States magnet plant runs out of helium on hand first. |
| 3 | Breaks first on the walled road. Drives the second mill lever. | In Future 3 this is the second part of the supply chain to fail. It is the reason Sourcing would qualify a second wire mill. |
| 3 | A node survives the screen only if it breaches the tolerance line. | We kept a site or supplier only if it could push the line past the 30-day limit. |
| 5 | Both are carried as watch items. | The resiliency team tracks both as watch items. |
| 5 | Any function that has not returned a number is asked again. | Ask again any function that has not returned its number by the end of 2026. |
| 6 | Encryption event / loss of systems / five weeks / C4 | A 35-day systems outage, such as ransomware |
| 7 | Four of seven indicators lean that way (while the table showed three). | Three of seven indicators lean that way. |

## 6. Source tags and confidence

Every claim says where it came from, and every judgement says how sure we are and why. The source for confidence is Intelligence Community Directive 203.

Use only these four source tags:

| Tag | Use it for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| AI-suggested | A fact read from public sources that nobody inside the OEM has confirmed yet | Superconducting wire comes from one qualified mill. |
| Panel judgement | A reasoning call made by the panel | In Future 1 the United States plant fails first, in the winter of 2028 to 2029. |
| Placeholder | Anything not done yet, including internal numbers nobody has confirmed | Days of helium on hand at the United States plant |
| Confirmed by (function), (date) | A fact a named function has checked | Confirmed by Site operations, Oct 2026 |

Status words such as Open, Settled and Watch item are separate labels, not source tags. The 12 Oct review removed invented tags such as "Unverified", "Not yet elicited" and "Placeholder decides".

Rules for confidence:

1. Confidence is high, moderate or low. No other levels, and no mixed levels such as "low to moderate".
2. The basis comes next to the level, in the form "Low confidence: a six to five vote, and days of helium on hand is a Placeholder."
3. No probabilities on futures. Likelihood words are allowed on specific events only, and never in the same sentence as a confidence level.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Low. Low to moderate. | Low confidence: the hazard facts are public and dated, but days of helium on hand is a Placeholder and the panel split six to five. |
| High that wire stays sole-sourced. | High confidence that wire is still design-locked in 2031: no magnet without it appears in US clearance records to Oct 2026. |
| Panel judgement, 11 of 11 on both axes | Panel judgement. All 11 members chose Question 1, and 10 chose Question 2. |
| Not yet elicited | Placeholder: interview not yet held |

## 7. Numbers and dates

Numbers follow AP conventions, and every number carries its basis. The basis is one of synthetic, estimate, Placeholder or a named public source.

1. Spell out one to nine in prose. Use numerals for 10 and above, and always for percentages, money, days and months.
2. Don't start a sentence with a numeral. Reword the sentence instead.
3. Write dates as 11 Oct 2026, and quarters as "the second quarter of 2027". Always include the year.
4. Write money as $12, not "12 dollars".
5. A number on a screen agrees with the same number everywhere else. If two run files disagree, show the conflict rather than picking one.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Eleven members. | The panel had 11 members. |
| 36 nodes went in, 10 survived. | The check kept 10 of the 36 sites and suppliers. |
| Up 3 days. | Up three days. |
| Decide at the April read. | Logistics decides at the April 2027 read. |
| Replace time: 36 | Months to replace: 36 (estimate) |
| About 12 dollars per cubic metre | About $12 per cubic meter |

## 8. Word list

Use exactly these terms on every screen. Spelling is US English, because the OEM's own category is "Labor".

| Future 2 | Trade costs rise and sealed magnets end the helium limit | F2, Shifted |
| Future 3 | A part is cut off and helium still limits shipments | F3, Walled |
| Future 4 | A part is cut off and sealed magnets end the helium limit | F4, Rewired |

## 9. Banned machine tells

These patterns make text read as machine-written. Each one appeared in the Lodestar material before the 12 Oct review.

| Tell | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Title fragments that promise structure | Five lines, then one decision. | MR conventional magnet line |
| Rhetorical setups and "not X, but Y" contrasts | Nothing here is a probability. | Each future shows what fails first and which way the indicators lean today. |
| "X, not because Y" flourishes | The hub never breaks first, because no future reaches it, not because the world spares it. | None of the four futures makes the hub fail first. The panel has not tested whether it is safe. |
| Punchy one-liners | Nobody reads eleven dossiers. | The screen shows one line per panel member, with the full prompt one click away. |
| Agreement framed as drama | Agreement is not the point of the panel. | (Deleted. The screen goes straight to the open questions.) |
| Middle dots joining fragments | Walled · drifting here | The indicators lean toward this future |
| Rule-of-three rhythm | What they expect, what they fear, what they would bet on. | Six questions on what the owner expects, doubts and would change their mind. |
| Clever verb-first labels | Breaks first / Hits hardest | First to fail |
| Metaphors standing in for facts | The cold head carries every magnet. / Niobium bites through the wire mill. | Once sealed magnets are most of output, losing the cold-head maker stops every shipment. / Niobium matters only through the wire mill. |
| Coined names | Walled, Rewired, Open, Shifted | Future 1 to Future 4, with their full names |
| Em dashes | (any) | A full stop, a comma or a colon |
| Chatty openers | Here's, Let's, Think of it as | (Start with the point.) |
| Text that describes the page | This page shows when the action has to start. | Sourcing must start by the second quarter of 2027. |

## 10. Checklist before publishing

Run every item on every page or screen, including ones you did not change. Quoted material is exempt from the wording checks, but must carry its quoted label.

- [ ] The name check passes: no client, brand or person is named.
- [ ] Every fact traces to a source, and nothing is invented to satisfy a rule.
- [ ] The first sentence on the screen is the bottom line.
- [ ] Every abbreviation is spelled out on first use on this screen.
- [ ] Every term matches the word list, with no codes on their own.
- [ ] Labels have no full stops, buttons are verbs, and nothing is in all capitals.
- [ ] Every claim carries one of the four source tags, and every confidence level has its basis.
- [ ] Numbers follow section 7 and agree with the same number elsewhere.
- [ ] A search finds no em dashes, middle dots or banned words: world, node, lens, axis, tolerance line, breaks, worldview, lever, signpost, drift.
- [ ] A rendered screenshot shows no clipped, overflowing or overlapping text.
- [ ] One read from top to bottom, as someone who has never seen the method, finds nothing that needs decoding.
